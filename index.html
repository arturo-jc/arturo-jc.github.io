Projects

Hardly Working

Electron · Angular · NgRx · SQLite · Drizzle

Alongside my full-time job I work as a contractor, which means billing my hours. Most trackers either make you start and stop a timer, which is easy to forget when you juggle several projects, or watch which windows are active, which is invasive and says little when most of your work happens inside a terminal and a browser. Hardly Working does neither. You link a project to a repo and a few URLs, and it records your edits and commits, reads your visits from the browser's own history (no extension needed), and reconstructs your hours from them. Nothing ever leaves your machine.

Step by Step Strength

Angular · Node.js · GraphQL · PostgreSQL · TypeORM

Step by Step Strength is a workout tracker for programs like Starting Strength, which alternate two workouts, A and B, three days a week. When I ran the program myself, the trackers I tried only supported weekly schedules, so a Monday-Wednesday-Friday plan repeated a workout across each weekend instead of alternating. SBSS supports schedules that span several weeks, so an A-B-A, B-A-B rotation is just a two-week schedule.

Research

Should the Number of Overlapping Experiencers Count?

Erkenntnis (2021)

What happens if we take someone's brain, and transplant each hemisphere into a different body? At the end of the operation, you have two people with equally good claims to be the original patient, and yet they can't both be the original patient. A well-known solution to this puzzle says "the original patient" was actually two people all along, who start out sharing one body and then go their separate ways. Now suppose two patients report an equally bad headache, and you have only one painkiller. If you find out one of them is scheduled for the operation, does that give you more reason to give them the painkiller? Intuitively, the answer is no: why would an operation that hasn't happened yet give you more reason to relieve someone's headache now? But if this theory is right, the patient awaiting surgery is really two people in pain. Some philosophers respond to this by saying that the two people share a single pain, so helping them relieves no more pain than helping the other patient. I argue that this response fails, because what matters is not just how much pain there is, but how many beings experience it. If the theory is right, you really should favor the patient about to be split, and the theory's defenders have to either bite that bullet, or find another way out.

Quantifier Variance, Ontological Pluralism and Ideal Languages

Philosophical Quarterly (2019). 69/275: 277–293

A lot of metaphysics is about the irreducible features of reality. Can facts about what is past, present and future be explained in more basic terms? Or is reality irreducibly tensed? Many philosophers understand such debates as debates over the "ideal" language: the language that best matches the structure of reality. From this perspective, to ask whether tense is irreducible is to ask whether the ideal language needs words like "was" or "will". This paper focuses on the relationship between the ideal language and its most basic terms. Is the language ideal because its terms are special, "carving nature at its joints"? Or are the terms special because they feature in an ideal language? The most common, atomist approach says that the terms come first. I argue that this approach struggles to distinguish two views about existence: ontological pluralism, according to which things can exist in different ways, and quantifier variance, according to which there are many equally good ways of talking about what exists. For this reason, I recommend a holistic approach, which puts the entire language first.

Duplication and Collapse

Thought: A Journal of Philosophy (2016). 5/3: 196–202

Composition as Identity (CAI) is the view that the whole is identical to its parts. Once you have fully described every part of, say, a person, you have fully described the person—or so goes the intuition behind it. Kris McDaniel argues that CAI is false because it rules out the possibility of strongly emergent properties: properties of a whole that are not fixed by its atoms. If, as some philosophers believe, consciousness were emergent, then a full description of a person's atoms would not tell you whether the person is conscious. Ted Sider replies that CAI has a strange consequence, which he calls collapse, that defuses this argument. Given CAI, there is no way to talk about a person's atoms and nothing else: any group that includes those atoms also includes everything they make up—hands, organs, etc.—up to the person themself. So phrases like "the person's atoms," which McDaniel's argument relies on, pick out nothing. In this paper, I argue that this response backfires. If collapse means you can't talk about a person's atoms on their own, it also means McDaniel's way of stating the intuition behind CAI doesn't capture it, since the intuition is about a whole's parts on their own. Defenders of CAI need another way to state it, and every obvious alternative rules out strong emergence again. They must give up either the intuition that motivated their view or the possibility of strong emergence.

Some Challenges to a Contrastive Treatment of Grounding

Thought: A Journal of Philosophy (2014). 3/3: 184–92

Why did Joe Biden win the 2020 US presidential election? Because he received an absolute majority of electoral votes. This is a constitutive explanation, not a causal one: the majority didn't cause Joe Biden to win—it is what his winning consisted in. Many philosophers posit a grounding relation underlying such explanations, which is standardly assumed to be transitive: if A is grounded in B, and B is grounded in C, then A must be grounded in C. Jonathan Schaffer has offered the following putative counterexample (among others): the fact that something is meowing is grounded in the fact that a particular cat, Cadmus, is meowing, and the fact that Cadmus is meowing is grounded in the fact that he came from a specific pair of sperm and egg (that's part of what makes it Cadmus meowing as opposed to some other cat), but the fact that something is meowing has nothing to do with Cadmus's origins. Schaffer's diagnosis is that grounding is contrastive, always a matter of "this rather than that," and that the chain breaks because the contrast quietly shifts in the middle. In this paper, I show how to tweak Cadmus's case to break the chain even when we hold the contrast fixed, and conclude that Schaffer's contrastive account can draw no support from the original case.
